The Policeman Doll

Piaget said children under seven can't see the world from someone else's point of view. Martin Hughes gave them a game of hide-and-seek instead. Drag the boy somewhere the policemen can't see him.

You can drag the policemen too, to try other hiding games.

What Piaget did

In the three mountains task (Piaget and Inhelder, 1956), children sat in front of a model of three mountains while a doll was placed at a different spot. They were shown pictures and asked which one showed the doll's view. Children under about seven usually picked the picture of their own view. Piaget called this egocentrism: being unable to take another person's perspective.

What Hughes did

Hughes thought the mountains task was strange and hard to relate to, so he designed a task that made "human sense" to young children: hiding from someone.

  1. Two small walls were set up to cross each other, making four sections.
  2. In a practice phase, one policeman doll was placed at the end of a wall. The child hid a boy doll where the policeman couldn't see him. Any mistakes were explained, and the child tried again until they understood.
  3. In the test, a second policeman was added. Now only one section was hidden from both of them, and the child had to find it.

To succeed, children had to work out what two different people could see from two different positions, and neither view was their own.

Around 90% of the 30 children found the correct hiding place, far younger than Piaget predicted they could take someone else's perspective. Even with more walls and a third policeman, most four-year-olds still succeeded. Hughes concluded that egocentrism largely disappears by about age four when a task makes sense to the child.

How convincing is it?

The study's big strength is its ecological validity: hiding from someone is something young children genuinely understand and care about, so it measures what they can really do. It also supports Donaldson's broader argument that Piaget's tasks underestimated children because they were confusing or unfamiliar.

But the two tasks aren't equally hard. Hiding only needs a child to judge whether someone can see a spot (a simple line of sight). The three mountains task asks them to picture exactly what someone sees and match it to a photo. The practice phase also corrected children's errors, so some may have been learning during the study rather than showing skills they already had. And a sample of 30 children is fairly small.

Evaluation at a glance

Strengths include a child-friendly, meaningful task, clear results and a well-controlled setup. Weaknesses include the easier nature of the task compared with Piaget's, the possibility of training effects from the practice phase, and the small sample.
